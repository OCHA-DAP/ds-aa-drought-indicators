Do temperature, rainfall and vegetation indicators predict drought impact?

A cross-country backtest for the ten countries where OCHA has an endorsed or in-development drought anticipatory-action framework: Afghanistan, Burkina Faso, Chad, El Salvador, Ethiopia, Guatemala, Honduras, Kenya, Mauritania and Niger. Every indicator behind our triggers, plus growing-season temperature, tested against real outcomes: national staple production (FAOSTAT, 2001 to 2024), CERF drought allocations dated to their deficit season, and EM-DAT drought events.

Bottom line. Our drought triggers key on rainfall and satellite vegetation. Across the ten countries, growing-season temperature is as good a predictor of staple-production shortfalls as the best vegetation index and better than any rainfall measure, and it is not what any framework uses. Pooled over 240 country-seasons, detrended temperature explains 13 percent of production variance out of sample; FAO's Agricultural Stress Index 14, mean VHI 13, ERA5 rainfall, SPI-3 and water balance 9 each, and ASAP's cumulative-FPAR biomass anomaly, the indicator two of our frameworks trigger on, 2 percent. Temperature and ASI together reach 19 percent and each keeps its own coefficient, so they carry different information. The strength is uneven: the signal is strong in the Sahel and Afghanistan, moderate in Chad and Kenya, and absent in Ethiopia and the Central American Dry Corridor, where national production does not follow the framework area's weather. Impact seasons are harder to rank than production because in several countries CERF has responded to drought in most years; where they are rare enough to test, temperature ranks them best in Burkina Faso, Niger, Chad and Afghanistan.

What was tested

  • Indicators, all from the JRC ASAP per-admin export (one consistent route, 2001 onward): temperature (ECMWF reanalysis, cropland), rainfall (CHIRPS), SPI-3, water satisfaction index (WSI), cumulative-FPAR anomaly (zFPARc, cropland and rangeland). Plus ERA5 monthly rainfall from the team database, and FAO GIEWS ASIS: the Agricultural Stress Index (share of cropland under stress in the season) and mean VHI, at admin 1. Temperature is detrended per unit (Theil-Sen, 1991 to 2025), biomass per unit (2001 to 2025), so that "hot" means hotter than that year would be expected to be.
  • Season and area. Each country's main growing season (dekads in data/config/countries.json); indicators averaged over the framework area and over the national mean of ASAP units. Biomass, WSI and SPI-3 are averaged over the last 60 percent of the season, where the trigger windows sit.
  • Targets. Continuous: national production of the country's staples (millet and sorghum in the Sahel, wheat in Afghanistan, maize and beans in Central America, maize in Kenya, all cereals in Ethiopia), as the percentage departure from a 2001 to 2024 linear trend. Binary: growing seasons with a CERF drought allocation dated to that season by the CERF supplement's rainfall-deficit period, or an EM-DAT drought event whose start follows that season. Undated chronic allocations are a sensitivity only.
  • Statistics. Ordinary least squares on standardised predictors with leave-one-out R² (the honest number with 24 seasons), AUC for the binary target, and a pooled regression with every series standardised within its country so that only within-country year-to-year variation is compared.

Reading the results

  • Where the weather explains the harvest, temperature is at the top. Mauritania (leave-one-out R² 0.54 nationally, every indicator strong), Afghanistan (0.38; ASI 0.60 in sample, SPI-3 0.53), Burkina Faso (0.23) and Niger (0.11, the strongest out-of-sample skill of any indicator there). In these four the hot seasons are the poor harvests and, where the impact record is sparse enough to test, the CERF seasons: AUC 0.72 to 0.80 for temperature in Burkina Faso, Niger, Chad and Afghanistan.
  • ASAP's biomass anomaly is the weakest indicator almost everywhere. Pooled leave-one-out R² of 0.02; negative in eight of ten countries. It comes close to temperature only in Mauritania, where every indicator works, and Chad, where neither does much. This is the indicator Burkina Faso and Chad trigger on. Its cousin from FAO, the ASI, does much better, likely because ASI counts the share of cropland under stress rather than averaging a z-score that carries a strong greening trend.
  • Rainfall is a weaker predictor than either heat or vegetation health everywhere except Kenya, where ERA5 rainfall over the long rains is the best indicator (in sample R² 0.34) and temperature adds little. Kenya's maize is grown in the highlands, not the arid counties the framework targets.
  • Nothing works in Ethiopia or the Dry Corridor. Ethiopia's national cereal production is a smooth growth curve dominated by highland Meher policy and area expansion; its residual has no relation to any indicator, and CERF has responded to drought in 15 of 24 seasons, so the binary target is saturated too. Guatemala, Honduras and El Salvador produce most of their maize outside the Dry Corridor departments, and the framework-area indicators have no relation to national production. For these countries the test needs subnational production, which is the data request this study points to.
  • Heat and vegetation stress are complementary. Temperature plus ASI is the best two-indicator combination in the pooled regression, and both coefficients stay significant; temperature plus biomass or plus rainfall adds nothing to temperature alone.

What this means for trigger design

  • A detrended growing-season temperature anomaly belongs in the candidate set for every drought framework's observational window, alongside ASI or VHI. It is available at the same time as the vegetation indices (ASAP updates every dekad), needs no new data agreement, and in the Sahel it is the single indicator most tied to outcomes.
  • Where a framework triggers on ASAP's zFPARc alone, the backtest against outcomes should be re-run with temperature and ASI as alternatives before the next revision. The Burkina Faso case is worked through on the Burkina site.
  • Temperature does not lead. On the Burkina Faso dekadal analysis heat coincides with dry spells rather than preceding them, so this is an in-season observational indicator, not a forecast-window one. The seasonal temperature forecasts have real skill, though, and a forecast temperature arm is worth a separate test.
  • The impact record is the weak link. Subnational production series from national statistics offices would allow the test at the scale the frameworks operate; the CERF supplement's season dating is what makes the binary target usable at all.

Caveats. National production against framework-area indicators is a scale mismatch, worst where the framework area is a small share of production (Ethiopia, Kenya, Central America). FAOSTAT production carries reporting noise and, in several countries, is itself partly estimated from weather. Twenty-four seasons per country; leave-one-out R² and the pooled regression are the numbers to trust, single-country p-values are indicative. The ASAP temperature series is ECMWF reanalysis-based; the team database's era5_temp table was checked as a cross-reference for Ethiopia and turned out to hold precipitation-like values, so it was not used. ASAP's per-admin export keys countries on a list position rather than its published ids; the probing that established the right ids is recorded in the repo. Impact-season dating rules are shown on each country page; a different convention moves a few seasons by one year. Detrending temperature removes the warming trend; the question of whether a fixed threshold would drift is the same as for biomass and is discussed on the Burkina site.

Robustness checks

A follow-up page, How robust is the temperature finding?, sets the result against the crop-climate literature, reruns the test on official province and district production statistics (FEWS NET Data Warehouse) for eight countries, and tries the GDHY gridded yields as a second target. Short version: the national ranking is reproduced by the official subnational statistics in Afghanistan, Niger, Mauritania and Burkina Faso and is consistent with the literature, but within individual provinces no indicator explains much of the year-to-year variation in official yields.
